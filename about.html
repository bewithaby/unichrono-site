About

A clock for people whose lives don't fit one timezone.

Unichrono is a world clock and widget app for Android and iPhone, made by Spiny Valley Labs. It is small, it is free, and it is built around a single promise: the time you glance at is always right.

Why Unichrono exists

It started with a widget that lied.

Most world-clock widgets redraw the time using background work. Android's battery optimisation, Doze, and the aggressive task killers that ship on many phones eventually shut that work down — and the widget quietly freezes at whatever time it last showed. You don't get an error. You get a wrong time you have no reason to distrust, right up until you call someone at 3 a.m.

That is a strange failure for a clock. A clock that is sometimes wrong is worse than no clock, because you stop checking.

Unichrono was built to prove the honest version was possible: a widget that runs nothing in the background, ticks straight from the system clock with each city's timezone attached, and therefore cannot freeze, cannot drift, and costs no battery. Everything else in the app grew out of that one idea.

The problem it solves

If everyone you care about lives in one timezone, you do not need this app. Unichrono is for the other situation:

  • Families split across countries. Is it too late to call Mum? Has Dad had his medicine yet?
  • Distributed teams. Which single hour on Thursday works for Berlin, Bengaluru and San Francisco without wrecking someone's evening?
  • Travellers. You land, your phone changes timezone, and suddenly every alarm you set means something different.
  • Anyone who does the arithmetic in their head — and gets it wrong twice a year, when one of the two places changes its clocks and the other doesn't.

The underlying problem is not arithmetic. It's that timezones are the wrong unit for a human question. You don't think "UTC+5:30". You think Mom. Unichrono lets you name a place after the person, then answers in those terms — on the home screen, in the planner, and in an alert that says the thing out loud.

The philosophy

The clock must never be wrong

Every design decision loses to this one. No background refresh, no cached "last known" time, no server that can go down. The widget reads the operating system's own clock and the timezone rules that ship with it, which is the same source your phone's lock screen uses.

Glance, don't unlock

Success is you never opening the app. The widgets and the notification are the product; the app is where you set them up and where the deeper tools live.

No account, no network, nothing collected

A clock does not need a server, so it doesn't have one. On Android the app does not even request the internet permission — it is technically incapable of sending anything anywhere. There is no analytics, no ads, no sign-in, nothing to breach and nothing to sell. That is a structural choice, not a policy promise.

Your names, your colours

The app adapts to you rather than the other way round: rename any city, set 12- or 24-hour time per taste, pick colours per widget so it matches your wallpaper instead of fighting it.

Free, and honest about it

Unichrono is free on Android and iPhone. No subscription, no ads, no feature held hostage. It is a small app that does one thing completely.

Who makes it

Unichrono is designed, built and maintained by Spiny Valley Labs, an independent one-studio operation. There is no growth team, no investor deck and no roadmap written by someone who doesn't use the app.

Practically, that means two things. Replies are human and occasionally slow. And the feature list is short on purpose — anything that would add a server, an account or a tracker does not get built, however often it is asked for.

City data comes from GeoNames.org, licensed CC BY 4.0. Timezone rules come from the IANA database that ships with your operating system.

Where it's going

The direction is depth, not surface area. Unichrono will keep being a world clock — it is not going to become a calendar, a chat app or a productivity suite.

  • Parity across platforms. A feature ships to Android and iPhone. The two apps should feel like the same product.
  • Better answers to "is now a good time?" The planner and the availability view are where the app is most useful and least finished.
  • More ways to glance. Lock screen, notification shade, quick settings — anywhere a time can appear without opening anything.
  • Still no server. If a feature needs one, it needs a very good reason, and it will probably lose.

What gets built next is mostly decided by what people ask for, which is the least ceremonious part of this whole thing: a form, no sign-in, straight to the person writing the code.
